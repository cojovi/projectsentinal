A screenplay passes from comedian to comedian. Each man dies. The next actor picks it up anyway. Eventually, someone locks the script in a safe.

As a horror pitch, the Atuk story is excellent. As a chain of evidence, it has some missing links.

The Ninjas Are Butterflies reel supplied for this review runs through John Belushi, Sam Kinison, John Candy, Chris Farley and Phil Hartman, presenting their deaths as a succession of ominous encounters with one script. The ending credits podcast episode 215 at 34:41. This review covers the supplied clip, not the full episode.

There really was an Atuk adaptation. There really were dead comedians associated with its history. But the reel makes the connections look tighter by compressing time, treating interest as casting and placing documentary-looking images beside claims those images do not establish.

That is how an old Hollywood legend becomes persuasive short-form content. You don't have to invent every fact. You can change what the facts appear to mean.

## A project became a pattern

Atuk was intended to adapt Mordecai Richler's 1963 novel, The Incomparable Atuk. The publisher describes an Inuit poet moving from Baffin Island to Toronto and embracing a society steeped in greed and pretension. The proposed film changed the geography to Alaska and New York.

Already, two versions of a story need separating. The reel folds the poet, a New York Times publication and the Americanized setting into one brisk origin story. That isn't a reliable summary of either the novel or the proposed movie.

The useful production record is less tidy. A January 1988 Los Angeles Times item announced a United Artists production starring Kinison. In February, the paper reported that the studio had sued him after eight days of shooting. Its November profile described a dispute over promised creative control, with Kinison and his former manager giving conflicting accounts.

Those articles document a film collapsing over work and money. Kinison's fatal crash occurred in 1992, not during that shutdown.

The reel alleges a later contract signed just three days before his death. The production reporting reviewed here does not substantiate that claim. It needs its own dated evidence; it cannot borrow credibility from the verified 1988 dispute.

## The calendar is doing inconvenient things

The clearest error comes near the end. The speaker says Hartman was killed two months after Farley died.

Farley died on December 18, 1997. Hartman was killed on May 28, 1998. The interval is 161 days, more than five months.

That correction does not make either loss less terrible. It changes the story's tempo. Two months sounds like a curse accelerating; more than five months gives you a different chronology. Kinison's production dispute and death are separated by years. Put those gaps back and the reel's apparent chain reaction becomes much harder to sustain.

Belushi and Farley did both die at 33. Their drug-related deaths also had a genuine similarity, although “the same overdose” needs care: the Associated Press report on Farley's autopsy identified cocaine and morphine, and compared it with the heroin-and-cocaine combination associated with Belushi's death.

Those similarities do not establish that a screenplay contributed to either death.

## Interest is not a signed contract

In 1999, the Los Angeles Times asked the screenwriter, Tod Carroll, about the legend. His account described Candy and Farley reading the script and expressing interest after the Kinison production fell apart.

That is narrower than the reel's sequence of accepted roles and imminent filming dates.

Hollywood projects can circulate through many people without financing, a completed deal or a scheduled shoot. Reading a script is one event; being contracted to star is another. If a retelling silently upgrades the first into the second, it makes the project seem closer to production each time tragedy intervenes.

The Farley-to-Hartman handoff appears in later histories as an allegation. We did not establish the clip's purported dialogue as a documented quotation. The claim that the script was finally locked in a safe is similarly unsupported by the reporting reviewed.

A safe is a satisfying final image. It is not a source.

## A newspaper-shaped image is not a citation

The clip mixes the hosts' conversation with portraits, newspaper imagery, a script on a desk and an empty film set. Those cutaways give the narration a historical texture.

An obituary can verify that someone died. It cannot, by itself, verify when he agreed to a role. A shot of script pages cannot prove the contract timeline. The newspaper imagery around the fictional poet does not establish a real New York Times publication.

We have not independently authenticated every visual, and appearance alone cannot tell us whether an image was AI-generated. The problem exists either way: a viewer can mistake an image that illustrates the narration for evidence supporting it.

This is a familiar failure in a feed. Captions, imagery and confident delivery arrive together, so their credibility gets bundled together too.

## The missing denominator

To assess a claim that reading a script kills people, you would need more than a list of deceased readers. You would need the full group of people exposed to it, their timelines and evidence distinguishing a script effect from unrelated causes.

The reel provides none of that. It expands “connection” from a lead actor to someone allegedly receiving a recommendation, while retaining only the tragic outcomes. That selection can create a striking pattern without demonstrating a cause.

Carroll offered a less cinematic explanation in the 1999 interview: “I think it's either coincidence or practical explanation.”

## Claim check

| Reel claim | Verdict | Evidence and limits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An Atuk movie entered production with Kinison | True | Contemporary 1988 reporting documents production and its shutdown. |
| Kinison filmed for nine days | Mostly true, but the count is wrong | Contemporary reporting says eight days. |
| A new contract preceded Kinison's death by three days | Unsupported | The reviewed production record does not establish that later signing. |
| Candy and Farley accepted the lead on the stated timelines | Unsupported as stated | Carroll confirmed reading and interest, not those contractual details. |
| Belushi and Farley died at 33 with similar drug combinations involved | Mostly true | The shared age is documented; the precise drug descriptions differ. |
| Hartman died two months after Farley | False | The documented dates are 161 days apart. |
| The script was locked away because it kills readers | Unsupported / speculative | No verified storage record or evidence of supernatural causation. |

Enjoy Atuk as folklore. Just don't let the reel substitute its editing rhythm for the calendar. The deaths are real; the curse is a conclusion the clip never earns.
